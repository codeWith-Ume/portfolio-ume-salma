DESIGNED ACTIVITIES

I enjoy designing activities that encourage people to think, express themselves, share experiences and learn from different perspectives. Here are three activities I designed around discussion, storytelling and personal reflection.

1. Thoughts into Words

Idea Behind the Activity

I designed this activity to encourage people to think beyond the surface meaning of statements, form their own opinions and support them with reasoning and real-life examples.

Topics

Participants explore statements such as:

  • Anger is not the problem, yelling is.
  • Disappointment is not the problem, blaming is.
  • Failure is not the problem, quitting is.
  • Disagreement is not the problem, disrespect is.
  • Jealousy is not the problem, controlling is.
  • Fear is not the problem, not trying is.
  • Mistakes are not the problem, repeating them is.
  • Competition is not the problem, cheating is.

How the Activity Works

  • Participants are divided into groups of five.
  • Each participant gets approximately 15 minutes to think about one topic.
  • Each person then gets 5 minutes to share their thoughts.

Discussion Questions

  • What does the statement mean?
  • Do you agree or disagree? Why?
  • Can you give a real-life example?
  • Have you ever experienced something similar?
  • What lesson can people learn from this statement?

Time Plan

StageTime
Explaining the activity10 min
Individual thinking15 min
Sharing thoughts30 min
Feedback5 min
Total60 min

What I wanted participants to explore: Critical thinking, reasoning, expressing opinions and connecting ideas with real-life experiences.

2. Stories That Speak

Idea Behind the Activity

I used short stories based on everyday situations to encourage participants to look at situations from different perspectives and reflect on how they respond to life's challenges.

Stories Included

The Snake

A person gets bitten by a snake but starts chasing it and asking why it bit them instead of seeking medical help.

Reflection: Sometimes we spend so much time asking "Why did this happen to me?" that we forget to ask "What should I do now?"

The Missed Train

A woman misses her train by one minute. Instead of checking the next train, she spends an hour arguing with the station staff and eventually misses another train.

Reflection: Sometimes, holding on to what has already happened makes us miss new opportunities.

The Flat Tyre

A man gets a flat tyre on his way to an important interview. Instead of changing it, he spends hours complaining about his situation and misses the interview.

Reflection: Complaining about a problem doesn't solve it; sometimes we need to take action.

The Dirty Window

A woman criticises her neighbour's laundry for looking dirty. One day, she notices that the clothes look clean. Her husband tells her that he had cleaned their window.

Reflection: Sometimes, the problem isn't what we are looking at but the perspective through which we see it.

The Gardener

A gardener gives every plant the same water, sunlight and care. Some flowers bloom while others don't. He understands that not everything is within his control.

Reflection: Sometimes, we have done our best, and we need to accept that not everything is in our hands.

Discussion Questions

After reading a story, participants reflect on:

  • What did you understand from this story?
  • Have you experienced something similar?
  • How did the story make you feel: inspired, guilty, peaceful, motivated or something else?
  • If you were the person in the story, what would you do differently?
  • Who would you like to share this story with?

What I wanted participants to explore: Perspective-taking, decision-making, emotional reflection and connecting stories with personal experiences.

3. Stories from Our Lives

Idea Behind the Activity

I designed this activity to give participants an opportunity to reflect on meaningful moments from their lives and understand how those experiences have shaped their thinking.

Topics

Participants choose a topic they feel comfortable sharing:

  • A childhood memory that shaped you.
  • A person or friendship that changed the way you think.
  • A decision or mistake that changed your direction.
  • A moment when you realised you were growing up.
  • A moment when you realised you wanted to help people.
  • A moment when you felt independent.
  • A moment when you understood something important about yourself.
  • A moment when you stood up for yourself.

Reflection Questions

  • What happened? What is the situation you want to talk about?
  • Why is this experience important to you?
  • How did you feel, and what were you thinking at that time?
  • What did you learn from this experience?
  • How has this experience changed the way you think, behave or understand others?

What I wanted participants to explore: Self-reflection, storytelling, listening, self-expression and understanding how life experiences shape us.

What These Activities Mean to Me

Through these activities, I explored how learning can happen through questions, stories, discussions and personal experiences. I enjoy designing activities where people are not just listening but actively thinking, expressing themselves and learning from one another.
